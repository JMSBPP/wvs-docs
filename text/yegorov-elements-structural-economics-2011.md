Elements of Structural Economics
Yuri Yegorov1
Received: 23 February 2010. Accepted: 1 December 2010
“All animals are equal, but some animals are more
equal than other.” (George Orwell “Animal Farm”)
Abstract. This article suggests some analytical approach to structures in economics
and initiates the development of interdisciplinary approach. Even brief classification
of socio-economic structures reveals the high complexity that is possible to tackle
only using different methodologies from different sciences. The article suggests that
physics, theory of network, synergetics and asymptotical theory can help to develop
mathematical version of institutional economics oriented on holistic approach. The
role of power and field for emergence of stable structures based on social interaction
is discussed. The formal model of power game with non self-regarding preferences is
considered as an example of modelling. Its social applications are also analysed.
Keywords: heterogeneity, socio-economic structure, power game, modelling.
1. Introduction
The goal of this article is to attract attention to structures in economics. It is a
contribution to the bridge between neoclassical and institutional economics.
Neoclassical economics is based on a set of assumptions (that can vary slightly from
model to model) and can derive the behaviour of agents in the market analytically.
This allows for qualitative and sometimes quantitative predictions. Institutional
economics deals with many complex structures in social life (the focus is on the role
of economic institutions) but is mostly descriptive and lacks mathematical
formalization. This allows it to deal with some problems that cannot be modelled
formally by classical economics. At the same time, it lacks the possibility to make
quantitative judgements.
Classical institutional theory goes from the works of Veblen (1899). It has moved
from law and politics to economics, and thus could not use mathematical
formalizations. Traditional neoclassical economics was a theory of individual agents
who interact via markets. Although institutes have not been a subject of research for
neoclassical economics initially, the theory moved towards an attempt to analyse
existing institutions using its classical tools. Institutional economics pays attention to
the structures that are formed by economic agents, such as household, firm and state.
Institutional economics departs from social norms and rules rather than property
rights; see, for example, Vanberg (1994). Brennan and Buchanan (1983) mention that

existence of multiplicity of equilibria, and even unique equilibrium might not coincide
with Pareto optimal point. Second, it allows for incomplete rationality suggesting not
only incomplete information (assumption consistent with neoclassical theory) but also
cognitive limitations (Olejnik, 2000).
This article can be viewed as an introduction to social meta-science that is not yet
born. It presents a set of different quantitative techniques developed by different
sciences (sometimes very far from economics) that can help to describe the
complexity of socio-economic interactions analytically, as well as to understand
limitations of this or that approach. The recent empirical analysis shows that modern
science is quite disaggregated. A special investigation about the shape of network of
the world scientific activity was done by Los Alamos National Laboratory (USA),
with the main results publishes in The New York Times. A new map of knowledge
based on electronic data searches was constructed2
. This map shows that economic
science has very few links with other sciences except for neighbouring sociology and
demographics. But real world economy is a complex system with very chaotic
behaviour at present. Physicists and other natural scientists are more accustomed to
work with complex systems. Development of interdisciplinary fields between social
and natural sciences can help us to understand the problems of real world better.
The focus of this article is not only on structures, but also on heterogeneities of
different origin that are responsible for their formation. This article is only a brief
sketch of complexity emerging in socio-economic structures. It is impossible to give
citations to all important works that might be cornerstones of such meta-science. That
is why references here are partly random and serve mostly as entry points to fields
and subfields that are less known to economists. It also presents a brief and obviously
incomplete summary of methods borrowed from different sciences that can deal with
this complexity analytically. Another goal is to show asymptotical limitations of
applicability of traditional quantitative social sciences for policy implications in real
world.

2. Structures in Economics and Methods of Their Analysis
Quantitative part of economics is represented mainly by neoclassical theory. Its main
assumptions include existence of agents (human individuals) with preferences that can
be either producers or consumers and interact via market. However, in order to start
production, these agents often need to create a firm. In order to supply public goods,
some kind of state should be created. Finally, even for own reproduction agents need
to create families. Clearly, these new objects (family, firm, state) represent some
structures that are as different from individual as molecule from atom. While the
whole (structure) is different from its parts, some sciences (like physical chemistry)
have succeeded in derivation the properties of the whole from the properties of its
parts.
2.1. Classification of structures
It makes sense to make some classification of economic structures taking into account
such factors as size, symmetry, strength, etc. Structures may be classified:
a) by size, small structure (family) versus large (nation);
b) by symmetry, symmetric structures (community) versus asymmetric
(hierarchy);
c) by strength of interaction - weak (easy to exit) versus strong.
Different types of structures require different analytical tools. While symmetric
structures can be analysed traditionally, asymmetric structures require network and
graph analysis.
Small structures have fewer features, and their analytical description is easier. For
example, it is easier to analyse strength of link in a family than in a firm. There are
only two key elements (wife and husband) and strength of link can be measured (by
frequency of divorce). Marriage organized by church is stronger than one by civil law,
if we observe divorces less frequently.
Structures can also be classified by involvement of agents (small or large fraction of
time). Each agent can belong to different structures, including different types of clubs
with high or low involvement.4
Structure becomes an independent unit that can take its own decisions, sometimes
different from decisions of at least part of individuals that this structure is composed
from. This is a very important question, and it leads to additional complexity. It is
possible to describe several types of methods of coming to joint solution. The first
method is voting; it assumes symmetry (here equal rights) of agents and thus
corresponds to democracy. An alternative method is by power coming from an agent
that occupies upper position in hierarchy. Later advantages and disadvantages of both
methods will be discussed in more detail.
There also exist two other possibilities. If all agents composing a group have identical
preferences, then group decision will not differ from individual decision. This
situation often takes place when individuals came from the same culture and tradition
or joined a club based on common preferences. However, this case is not so common.
Another possibility (considered by game theory) is related to bargaining. It reflects
some asymmetry of agents and is related to such division of shares of common pie
emerging from joint activity that allocation is stable given common knowledge of
alternative options and time preferences for all agents. This approach became so
popular in neoclassical economic theory that even family relations are often modelled
using this approach. However, adding some irrationality (emotions, for example) can
easily destroy such equilibrium. Emerging quarrels are likely to destroy family
relations, while work on preference evolution to make them more similar can increase
the stability of a family.
2.2. Limitations of modern economical theories
Although neoclassical and institutional economics are still relatively isolated islands
of knowledge, they both lack one direction of research: emergence of structures.
Indeed, institutional economics considers structures as given and focus its research on
its particular properties. For example, at the level of a firm research may be focused
on contracts as relations between principal and agent, on one hand, and legal
implementation of the force that keep agents on firms together, on the other hand.
Although neo-institutional theory has the set of rigid axioms to explain economic
behaviour of human individuals, these axioms do not allow for derivation of
endogenously emerging structures. Indeed, neoclassical theory was successful to
explain even relations in family; see Becker (1976). New institutional theory has a
different approach. Similarly to regional science, it uses different approaches for
solving different practical problems. Game theory becomes its tool quite frequently,
but games can vary from standard (based on rational approach) to evolutionary (based
on incomplete rationality and even automata behaviour).
The question that is interesting for us can be formulated as follows. What forces of
interaction have to be introduced that relatively stable groups of individuals (families,
firms, nations, states) will emerge? Such problem has been already studied by Engels
(1884). His research embraced the role of evolution and traditions (more common for
institutional rather than neoclassical economics) but it lacked any quantitative and
analytical description (tools used by neoclassical economics). Bowles (2003)
addresses many institutional issues but uses quantitative approach (like evolutionary
games).5
It seems that construction of such a theory requires substantial departure from basic
methodology of both neoclassical and institutional economics, and it is not very likely
to take place. That is why it makes sense to make an excursion to a science that has
some successful experience in dealing with such problems. This science is physics.
While it has no unique methodology (even the general theory of field is still absent), it
contains many self-consistent formalized theories based on sets of formal axioms
(assumptions) and applicable under certain parameter limitations. Examples include
thermodynamics applicable for gases, and Newton mechanics applicable for velocities
well below the speed of light. At the same time, physics develops itself using pioneer
methods (each problem specific) both theoretical and empirical when it deals with
actual problems that cannot be explained by axiomatic theories.
2.3. Levels of complexity
The complexity of both real world and its models emerges at several levels. As any
other science, economics deals with elements (agents) and links between them.
Topologically, such structure can be presented in a form of graph (analysis of such
graphs is done by theory of networks, that will be considered below). In the simplest
case the graph structure is neglected; this corresponds to symmetric links between all
agents and is implicitly assumed in most of the models of market interaction.
However, agents can be heterogeneous. This heterogeneity can manifest itself in two
ways: asymmetry between elements and asymmetry between links. Thus, we can
arrive to different levels of complexity in modelling that is summarized below.
Level 1: identical agents with symmetric costless access to the market that is the
unique area of their interaction (neoclassical economics).
Level 2A: heterogeneous agents (differ in preferences, wealth, etc) that join market
symmetrically.
Level 2B: identical agents join market heterogeneously.
Level 3: heterogeneous agents have heterogeneous links to the market.
Level 4: heterogeneous agents with heterogeneous links interact not only via market
force.
Level 5: forces leading to emerging of structures that glue agents like atoms are glued
to molecules; interaction between agents and such structures.
Heterogeneity of links can also be of several types:
a) Spatial grid where distance matters (regional science and urban economics);
b) Spatial grid where only topology of links matter (theory of networks).
2.4. Analogies from physics
It may be useful to compare the present status of economic science (as a set isolated
islands of knowledge) with physics at earlier stages of its development (17-18th
centuries), when only some phenomena had mathematical formalization while others
have been studied mostly by experiment. In physics, the laws of gas dynamics have
been known well before some formal models of condensed matter.
At present, neoclassical economics has quite advanced set of mathematical tools but
deals mostly with atomic agents interacting only via markets. This description is quite
similar to gas in physics (see Yegorov, 2007). Indeed, gas is the simplest structure in
its modelling, since kinetic energy of atoms is much larger than potential energy of
interaction, and the latter may be neglected. At the same time, the behaviour of matter6
in liquid and solid states cannot be modelled without explicit account for interaction
between atoms. At the further level of complexity, atoms themselves are not stable.
Interaction between them leads to emergence and decomposition of molecules, the
subject of physical chemistry.
It seems that deep analogy between social and natural sciences can exist. The role of
structures in economics is quite important. Institutional economics considers many
structures (social institutions) and even neoclassical economics takes some of them as
given (firms, for example). But what is a firm? There exists a whole theory of firms,
but it concentrates on different aspects of its behaviour. For example, industrial
organization considers interaction of firms in the market, while institutional finance
focuses of different methods to attract capital (by shares or debt).
If we look at one individual and his role in a firm, we have to employ some tools from
economics of networks, because each individual occupies different hierarchical
position in a firm, and his power and wage depends on this position. In theory, one
may think about organization of firms on democratic principles (when team and
leadership are formed for each task afresh based on democratic principles of majority
in voting, but we rarely can see this implemented). The reason is that we need some
structural rigidity for a firm to be competitive in the market. This rigidity comes from
internal interaction inside a firm, and this interaction is quite different from
interaction of producers and consumers in the market.
Historically, we have also observed the significant role of power for interaction of
agents. Kingdoms and corresponding hierarchies existed for many centuries. The
democratic principle of social life, although popular today, has less weight through
the history. And still even in democratic states this principle does not dominate inside
firms.
Thus, we see that modern society is quite asymmetric. One asymmetry coming from
difference in wealth can be potentially eliminated in egalitarian societies via
redistribution. But there is also another type of asymmetry. Even if all agents would
have identical skills and identical wealth, they still might have different power. Why?
First, we see that hierarchies are necessary for social existence, because of
coordination principles, enforcement of state and imposition of social order.
Hierarchies can be described by networks. These networks are similar to molecules in
chemistry: two identical atoms have different properties because of difference in their
location in a network.
2.5. Asymmetry and Networks
In fact, asymmetry exists in the markets as well (although it is often neglected in
formal models). It is often assumed that agents have symmetric and costless access to
the market.
But this is not true. Consider a pure economic model when only market relations
between agents are considered. Let us consider a gas pipeline. It links producer(s) and
consumer(s) that are located in (substantially) different geographical points. The
construction of such pipeline is a substantial fraction of total cost to produce and
deliver this good (natural gas) to consumer. A natural problem emerges: who will pay
to link producer and consumer together? If we have several distinct points and several7
pipelines (with different cost of delivery), the asymmetry between different market
participants becomes even more pronounced. Due to high cost in construction, local
monopoly (for supply and often for transit) becomes an essential part of such
problems. Thus, to model such a market we can no longer use a standard neoclassical
assumption of symmetric and costless access of all producers and consumers to the
market. Thus, economic forces should be complemented by geographical and political
(Yegorov, Wirl, 2010).
There might be two asymmetries: among individuals and links between them. Even if
we assume all individuals to be equal, asymmetry can be introduced by the shape of
links between them. Such structures are studied by theory of networks that assumes
nodes (agents) and links being equal and focuses on different topologies of connecting
nodes into network. A good introduction to this theory might be the book of Duncan
Watts (2004). Watts & Strogatz (1998) describe what properties of networks are
interesting for scientists. The connection topology can be either completely regular or
completely random. Interestingly, some properties of networks are statistically stable
for intermediate case. If one “rewires” regular network to introduce some disorder, it
is possible to arrive to a class of “small-world” networks. “Small worlds” are highly
clustered like regular lattices but have small characteristic path lengths like random
graphs. They exhibit the property of “six degrees of separation”, a statement that the
average path (who knows whom) between two people in the world is not much
different from 6 intermediate nodes. Many real networks, both physical (like power
grid) and social (like collaboration graph of film actors) exhibit this “small-world”
property.
While initial analysis of network properties has been done by mathematicians,
physicists and computer scientists, recently this field has attracted the interest of
economists. In fact, spatial games are studied both by physicists and economics. To
give some link, two references on work from both scientific groups will be provided.
Czabo and Fath (2007) study evolutionary games on graphs. When interacting agents
are linked in social network, the solution concepts and methods are very similar to
those from non-equilibrium statistical physics. The major interest of researchers is
about the influence of graph structure of interactions on the picture of long term
behavioural patterns emerging in evolutionary games.
Calvo-Armengol and Jackson (2004) theoretically analyse the importance of social
networks for labour markets. There are empirical findings that about 50% of jobs are
found through social contacts. Consider two networks, one being superior in giving
agents higher expected future income stream. These two structures (groups) are
initially asymmetric. Gents from “bad” network have more incentives to drop, and
their decision has contagion (cascade) effect on other participants.
It is possible to draw a network with nodes at the locations of producers and
consumers (can be drawn as balls of different colours) and links between them. Such
topological structure reflects asymmetric relations between agents. If agents are
producers and consumers, then such asymmetry may take account of asymmetric
access to information (for goods and services traded not via free market, but via
private connections; it was quite typical in the former USSR when under shortage of
some goods it was possible to purchase it using private links and paying additional fee8
to provider). The asymmetry may also come from difference in geographical
locations, but here the cost of market access (transport costs, fees and duties) is better
modelled using approaches from regional science.
Network approach is also useful when agents occupy different positions in social
hierarchy. The number of connections to other agents may serve as measure of market
power. Different topologies of networks also play role. Such models are often
analysed by econo-physicists, since they have more experience in mathematical
modelling of networks.
2.6. Methodology
There are many different aspects of analysis of structures. Here some of existing
approaches will be listed.
For any simple structure, we already have two levels: individual and collective. It is
good to have formal mathematical technique that can describe them on the same
language. In neoclassical economics, micro and macro models can in principle be
written in similar terms, after the procedure of aggregation. (This procedure is similar
to derivation of equations of statistical physics on the basis of mechanical system for
micro world).
An important class of structures is spatial. Economic agents can be mobile and
immobile in space, and for different purposes their mobility (or immobility) can be
neglected. When the problem is in choosing location for residence or work, then we
deal with such spatial structures as cities. Since all agents cannot physically live in
one location, natural asymmetry between their locations emerges. For example,
central business district (CBD) theory derives equilibrium land rent that has to
compensate otherwise identical agents for disutility to travel more to his work. CBD
theory is an important (but not unique) part of urban economics, while regional
economics deals with a high variety of spatial structures, taking into account
interaction between geography and economics in a quantitative way.
Such meta-science as synergetics3
studies the issue of emergence of structures and
their self-organization. It considers both natural and social structures and focuses on
some general principles of transforming parts into the whole, which is not simply sum
of its parts. Synergetics typically uses such mechanisms as nonlinear dynamics and
positive feedback. These mathematical tools describe the system out of equilibrium
and are good for mathematical description of emergence. Arthur (1994) had used
these ideas to show the emergence of economic structures.
It is also possible to formulate some questions that deserve further analysis. The first
issue is related to ecology of structures, i.e., conditions for their growth and/or
survival. The second issue is social optimality. What level of structural link (or
hierarchy) is better for the society? If the level of integration of individuals into
structures is small, it may lead to loss in competition across large firms, but it is good
for already wealthy individuals, as they face less oppression. If the level is high, there
is a chance to win competition of world powers, and to have one-polar world. But for
intermediate stage of bipolar world there was a risk of mutual annihilation (Cold war).

An important issue: methodologies of what sciences can be borrowed by social
scientists for the solution of practical problems related to social structures? Here a lot
depends on what we want to know about structures. Universal approach might be
never found, and definitely not at the initial steps. Two useful approaches (theory of
interaction, or field, in physics, and theory of networks) have been already mentioned.
It is also important to draw attention to asymptotical approach in modelling. Roughly
speaking, some complex system can be described as a set of equations that depend
also on some small parameter that can be neglected in some cases. The key modelling
issue is to know when we can neglect which effect as secondary. This method is well
known in natural sciences and applied mathematics (see, for example, Nayfeh
(2000)), while in social sciences it was used by Barantsev (2003).
Let some system is described by non-solvable (or even non-writable) system of
equations that depends on vector x and some small parameter 1: F(x, )=0. Then in
higher order it can be reduced to simplified (solvable) system F0(x,0)=0. When   ∞,
there may be another limit model. For example, if  is the parameter, describing the
ratio of potential energy of interaction to kinetic (individual) energy in physics, then
for   0 we have the limit of gas state of matter, while for   ∞ we have the limit of
solid body. The formal model for both states is much easier than for intermediate
(liquid). There may be several small parameters, with different limit models when
each of them is infinitely small. In social science, there might be some general theory
(at present even not formalized) that can have classical microeconomics as one limit
(gas society) and the theory of class struggle of Marx in some other limit (when social
interactions “glue” society into two antagonistic groups).
Asymptotic approach can link together the models developed by different branches of
science, or even by different sciences. In physics, thermodynamics (gas theory) and
theory of condensed state are two different sub-fields. There may be no chance to
develop unified theory based on axiomatic approach (this is what neoclassical
economics attempts to do). It may be better to build science from different bricks,
oriented on description of particular problems, like it is done in regional science, for
example.
The (not yet developed) theory of condensed matter in economics may be useful since
it may predict economic effects of policies more accurately. For example, pumping
the budget with money can do positive effects, when society needs a lot of public
goods and when bureaucracy is not corrupted. If it is corrupted, we can have effect
similar to low sensitivity of volume change in solid body to heating (comparing to
gas). Lion share of this money can go to personal enrichment of bureaucracy
(accumulation of internal energy) without substantial social effect. However, liberal
market can do the job even worse. The solution might be to study the conditions for
“superconductivity”, i.e. when bureaucracy becomes honest and efficient.
3. Forces that Bring Structures Together
In physics, there are free particles (atoms) and forces of interaction between them.
Modern physics distinguish between gravity, electromagnetic, strong and weak
interactions. All of them are described by some fields that have mathematical10
expression. For social interactions, not only material but also ideal links (related to
information) are important. What social forces can glue elements (agents) into one
structure? If we abstract from biological drive (like attraction between sexes that also
plays an important role for family formation) and focus on more rational, it is possible
to distinguish across two conceptually different forces. The first one is common
interest, and the second is power. Attali (2006) suggested that there might be three
ruling orders in society: based on economic market (power of gold), based on brutal
force (military power) and based on religion (or ideology).
3.1. Power
The concept of power is attracting more interest of researchers today. While in
economics its role is marginal (something like market power of monopoly), its role
for the social sciences is high. Without trying to be fully correct, let us define power
as some asymmetric relationship between individuals, where one individual can
impose his will over another (then we say that he has power). At the first glance, this
contradicts all axioms of market relationships in economics, where all individuals can
rationally choose among available options to maximize their utility. How any power
can come here? There are at least three possibilities:
a) Power can restrict the set of available options (for example, for a prisoner),
b) Power can change the mechanism of individual choice (changing individual
rationality or preferences via religious or ideological influence)
c) Power can act as an independent force that is complementary to market force.
Thus, there is the first contradiction: power is an enemy of Homo economicus, but it
might be necessary for the stability of social structures that are optimal for the society
(a kind of contradiction between individual and group interests).
Where the power comes from? We can see power interaction even among groups of
animals, where it is often based on brutal force. Similar has been observed in
primitive human societies, but is not consistent with modern principles of morality
and democracy (unless powerful action is imposed by state and law over criminal).
Since this article is not only about power, let us focus only on its interaction with
social structures.
While there is a trend in economic literature about deriving all social relationships on
the basis of microeconomic principles, the emerging equilibria are often unstable.
Would we indeed believe that stability of every family depends on Nash bargaining?
In reality, there is also force of attraction (completely non-economical in origin) and
maybe external costs imposed by society (like costly divorce), that makes this
structure robust.
In reality, economic agents are asymmetric not only in their wealth, but also in
physical force, but the latter has been proxy for power only in primitive societies.
Later magic knowledge gave more power, and similar power has been used later by
priests. At certain moments, military power was dominant in particular states,
especially empires. Until the last decades, economic power was only complementary
to these alternative powers. But even if we depart from real societies and will make an
exercise about constructing optimal society, we will see that although in many cases
power only makes abuse, in certain cases it is necessary. For example, we can impose
social structure that would eliminate negative externality, otherwise naturally11
emerging from free market interaction of agents. Section 4 will present a formal
model related to powerful relationships in society.
3.2. Emergence
If all individuals are free to join or exit any structure, they would form an ideal
society without any oppression. Indeed, if there would be no power (understood as
force influencing actions of an individual that are different from freely chosen), one
can imagine a chain of joining of different elements into different (or possibly
common) structure so that after each step the situation becomes Pareto improving.
Finally we can end with Pareto optimal structure, and it would be stable without any
oppression.
It is easy to model such ideal situation formally. Suppose that we have a production
function with scale economies: Y=N1+a
, where a>0, N – number of agents in structure
(let us name it a firm), and Y is output. Then the output per capita y=Y/N= Na
will
grow with N, and everybody in this firm will be better off if additional agent joins it.
In the end we would have all agents in one firm (structure), and this is Pareto optimal
outcome.
If the production function is S-shaped, i.e., has some range of economies of scale
followed by decreasing returns to scale, then such growth will be stopped at some
stage, and we will observe decomposition of all agents into several firms of optimal
size. Similar situation takes place for spatial allocation of agents. With S-shaped
utility from living in a city of size N, we will end with several cities of optimal size. If
scale economies are such, that decreasing returns do not show up before all population
comes to one city, we have the situation of mega polis growing without limits
(Mascarilla-i-Miro, Yegorov, 2005).
So far, we do not need any power. But why do we observe it in reality? Can it be
eliminated, or do we need it for something? If we do not need, then there potentially
exists anarchic society with Pareto optimal allocation and no oppression. Some
idealists believed in such society, but is has been never implemented for substantial
period.
Let us look into the history to see how powerful structures have been emerging. A
tribe living in isolation several thousand years ago (and some living now in isolated
areas like Amazonia) can be organized into optimal groups with different
specialization, reaching the highest productivity and applying principle of equality in
distribution (a kind of primitive communism). However, population on Earth was
growing, and it was impossible to live in full isolation. Collision of two tribes could
result in a war, and it became necessary to organize army for provision of such public
good as security. In primitive societies sizes of tribes were small, and the size of army
could be several dozens of warriors at most. In such small group it was possible to
find most skilled leader of army, who can even be elected by unanimous vote4
.

army; the last example is Russia in 1917 when soldiers started to elect their
commanders and to decide whether to continue war (the First World War) or to go
home. The front dissipated, and thus democratic organization in army has been proven
to be less efficient than hierarchy.
Other examples of hierarchies include large production and territorial management
(government structure in relatively large states). Summarizing, it is possible to say
that hierarchies were born from the need to govern and manage large groups of
agents, either for production or for defence.
3.3. Why tensions and conflicts?
We observe a lot of inefficiencies in contemporary world. One of them is excessive
level of tension and conflicts. They are observed on different levels: inside firms,
inside countries and in international relations. These conflicts lead to loss in
resources, often quite substantial. Thus, such cases seem to represent a kind of
inefficient outcome, and it makes sense to study the origin of this inefficiency.
Contemporary economic theory tries to explain all social outcomes on the basis of
underlying economic driving forces. Microeconomic theory focuses more on
preferences, optimization and different types of equilibria, while evolutionary theories
use other concepts, including evolutionary games.
The goal is to analyse the origin of tensions on the level inside structure. If structures
are not rigid and agents have a freedom to join or to quit them, such tension that
perturbs individual freedom is a bad that should be avoided if possible. In the
language of neoclassical economics such perturbations are typically known as
externalities. Institutional economics analyses different roles of states. Buchanan
(1975) distinguishes between two roles of state: protecting and producing. The first
function is the result of social (typically constitutional) agreement between society
members. Existence of state leads to emergence of some rules of behaviour (social
norms, laws) that somehow limit individual freedom. However, full destruction of
state typically leads to less efficient outcome. In some cases state may develop into
oppressive structure, and this causes the rise of anarchism. However, anarchists can
only destroy but do not have a theory how to create more efficient state. This means
that for efficient social coexistence agents have to give up part of their individual
freedom for the common. It would be easier if agents would have not purely
individualistic but also collective preferences. This issue of non self-regarding
preferences will be discussed later.

as he did for the organizing role of morality and state. However, only market theory
has been developed later into highly sophisticated mathematical model. But the lack

current level of development of social sciences does not give us a right to neglect
these other components. The collective interaction of agents leads to formation of

3.5. About non self-regarding preferences
While preferences for consumption are non-uniform (differ across agents), they are
normally self-regarding, i.e., agents normally do not care what other agents consume.
However, consumption does not cover all aspects of social life. Psychologists
consider also different aspects of social desires, like security, respect, power, sexual
drive, etc. Some of these desires can be satisfied in non-market relations, and thus can
be considered apart from economic theory. For example, in normal society sex should
be free good, if the desire of both sides is equal6
. Sometimes security and respect can
be bought on some market, but this does not affect preferences of others. However,
drive for power (over other people) is such a component of preferences that cannot be
self-regarding. Envy and deprivation also belong to such types of preferences where
agents care about preferences of others. Preferences can also be altruistic when agents
are happy if somebody near them is happy.
While the majority of economic theories still consider preferences as self-regarding,
sociologists started to depart from these assumptions. For example, McClintock
(1978) defines complex axis, keeping individual preferences towards himself on real
axis, while his attitude to preferences of others at the orthogonal axis. If this second
component is positive, preferences contain a component of altruism, but negative
attitude looks not normal (deriving utility from suffering of others is not excluded,
although it describes a kind of misanthropic behaviour).
In order to explain emergence and survival of altruistic behaviour (that definitely
lacks rationality in the framework of Prisoner’s dilemma game), different authors
used different mechanisms like interaction on a spatial grid and competition between
groups; see Bowles (2003).
The preferences different from self-regarding might give a richer class of economic
models that can explain real social behaviour. Some initial ideas about possibility to
describe Marxist (egalitarian) or nationalist society using this tool have been
expressed by Yegorov (2007).
While negative non-self-regarding preferences have not been used so far in economic
models, there are studies about deprivation (see Stark & Taylor, 1991), when
individual care not only about their absolute welfare but also about their relative
welfare towards other agents. This approach seems to be promising, and will be also
used in modelling of this paper. In fact, such preferences U=u+ik(u-v) are
mathematically equivalent to V=u-iw, with negative angle =arctg (-u/w), resulting in
a mix of individualistic and moderately misanthropic behaviour.

for interdisciplinary advance. First, we have to admit the possibility of a conflict of

Is it possible to overcome such difficulties? In principle, the answer is positive.
Similar to physics, it is necessary to describe the field (as some interaction between
individuals) that leads to structure (emergence of group). But to do this, one should
deviate from description of agents that interact only economically: such group will be
virtual and quite unstable. To keep groups (or institutional structures) stable one need
to introduce “power”, some external force that is created to keep institutions stable in
order to satisfy the interests of majority. Thus, even in democratic societies we need
some oppression. However, power that has been created initially to support social
interests can have its own life, and to create structures that are socially suboptimal.
One possibility is a shift to autocracy, where the society no longer acts in the interests
of the majority. Here we turn to completely different area of study of social sciences.
But already shift to “power” as force complementary to market, is a substantial
deviation from neoclassical economics. Moreover, for successful analysis of emerging
array of complex phenomena more deviations are likely to be needed: deviation from
rationality principle (rationality of individual and social structure may be not
consistent), similar access to information (societies often ration information to their
members to keep social stability) and much more.
The next question is whether connections inside a group are symmetric or
asymmetric. Symmetric contacts of N-size group assume N(N-1)/2 interactions. In the
past, for large N this was simply impossible technically as people lived in different
places. The development of telephone, later mobile and internet connection eliminated
one technical obstacle. Nevertheless, complexity of coordination of joint activity
called for a possibility to create hierarchic structures. Scale economies in management
call for saving on the number of links. This leads to asymmetric topologies similar to
branching of a tree or road network. However, as purely technical solution (for
example, project manager does not communicate with 1000 workers directly, but
through a chain that has 10 upper bosses, each of which has 10 lower level bosses,
that communicates each with 10 workers directly) this introduces asymmetry in a
group. Is it possible to keep such order stable? Will it bring some negative social

4. Theoretical Formulation of Power Game
The goal of this section is to consider two stylized games that can explain relations
between two agents with asymmetric power. People are not equal not only in their
preferences, but also in skills, where energy and drive for power plays an important
role. Gumilev (1989) suggested the view on the development of different nations that
is based on the fraction of such people with power drive, that he named “passionary”.
In this section a stylized game will be proposed to show that some level of conflict is
equilibrium when powerful agent has not self-regarding preferences, i.e., values
power (or relative position) in addition to valuation of usual payoff. Here the concept
of conflict means any level of confrontation, including psychological, and the loss of
value might be indirect, via the loss of utility or productivity.
4.1. Game 1: Self-regarding preferences
The following assumptions are introduced. Let w (0<w<1) characterizes the level of
tension in the society (sometimes is also referred to as the level of conflict, or war).
There are two players: leader (X) and follower (Y). The leader can have two
strategies: peace (P) and conflict (C). Moreover, he can choose the conflict level (w)
out of the continuum of values. The follower can respond as Hawk (fight) or as Dove
(accommodate). One of the crucial assumptions is exogenous asymmetry in powers of
X and Y: while in peaceful coexistence their economic powers are comparable
(identical, for simplicity), in the case of conflict X has dominant power. Thus, we call
this situation “one-polar world”. Due to power asymmetry it is natural to assume X as
a leader and to give him the possibility to select a game by choosing w.
Suppose that in the state of peace (strategy chosen by X) both players get one unit of
payoff. Thus, we can introduce a pie of size 2, equally split between players. The state
of conflict has two effects:
a) it leads to pie size shrinking to 2(1-w);
b) it leads to new shares in pie splitting in favour of X, depending on conflict
intensity w.

5. Applications
In hierarchical societies the player X can be associated with “boss”, who normally
leads the group not only by setting commands but also by having possibility to
punish. In a dynamic set up, some new players can evolve to the power level that can
be comparable with boss. Thus, in order to preserve his power, boss sometimes
demonstrates it to keep confidence of everybody about the power structure. If a
competitor in power emerges, boss may lose power struggle, and institutional
structure can change. While new winner is most likely will replace the boss and keep
hierarchical structure, we can also consider the emergence of new structure (for
example, democracy) that can lead to decomposition of the whole hierarchical
staircase. Since democracy has no power fight, it is more efficient in production.
Thus, competition of democratic groups with hierarchical can win economically. Of
course, a democracy should be able to defend itself in an open conflict if necessary.
Such fight for power is also observed in the societies of animals. For examples, young
cocks fight with each other (and many eventually die) so that in the end the strongest
cock maintains harem of hens. Example of swans (with monogamy relations) shows
that even for birds this is not always the rule. At the same time, we observe hierarchy
among some monkeys. Evolution has shown that hierarchic relations not always
dominate. Thus, hierarchy is not a superior equilibrium, but only one of possibilities
that survives certain evolutionary pressure.
The human relations under certain pressures also end in hierarchy with different level
of conflict. Further the cases of relation type (with focus on conflict game that helps
to maintain hierarchy) will be considered.
5.1. The level of firm
As it has been discussed above, large firms cannot be competitive if all decision are
taken democratically, and thus contain some level of hierarchy. Although hierarchy
has the primary goal to raise the effectiveness of management, any leader (boss)
naturally gets some level of power in the leaded group that can be often used for his
own benefit (to raise the share of pie).
At the firm’s level, some kind of hierarchical structure typically exists. Exceptions are
small groups with scale economies or positive externalities from collaboration, when
democratic relations are more efficient. Typically, a boss gives orders to a group of
collaborators. However, his skills might be not always superior. In order to maintain19
his power over longer period, he may introduce low level of conflict in a group in
order to kill in the bud potential rivals for his power. If this conflict level is not
marginally small (and it can be small, only if boss has no rivals, i.e. his skills are
superior), then it leads to a substantial decrease in efficiency.
If we have a combined within-group and between-group competition, then those
groups will show better performance (and thus survive in the long run) where the
level of conflict is smaller, and the position of boss is really superior and stable.
However, we often observe excessive level of conflict inside firms, and some
measures should be done for its decline and efficiency increase.
5.2. The level of country
Similar situation may occur at country’s level. Again, here we do not consider normal
democracies, where several rival parties can have similar power and nobody’s power
has high stability. Consider the case when in a more autocratic regime some
hierarchical vertical power is constructed. It is well known that votes can be
influenced by different kind of manipulations, and also by the so called
“administrative effect”, when an incumbent supported by ruling power not only has
more resources to win elections, but also when image of his rival is destroyed. The
typical situation here is almost one-party vote (with majority well above 60%) when
rivals are suppressed and end with only few per cents of votes. For more democratic
participants and observers this administrative resource can be perceived as moderate
level of conflict, leading to implementation of incumbent’s power (even when he
cannot win honest competition).
Russian history of the 20th
century presents examples of possibilities to have different
social orders. The fall of monarchy in February 1917 has led to emergence of many
political parties9
that suggested different plans for future development. The book
(Veller, 2007) gives their description based on less biased views on history comparing
to the époque of the USSR. In spring and summer of 1917, there was the period of
two powers: Temporary government (with constitutional democrats in majority) and
Soviets. Bolshevicks, leading party later on who based their ideology on the
dictatorship of workers over other social classes, has been in minority even in Soviets,
where majority belonged to socialists-revolutionaries, who defended the interests of
peasants, vast majority of the population. The ideology of anarchism also played an
important role that time. They rejected the state but they supported self-organized
communes of peasants and also viewed the future of cities as factories owned by
communes of workers. Unfortunately for Russia, this happened in the middle of the
First World War, and there was no chance for long and peaceful competition between
these ideologies. In fact, anarchists also used physical power to rob rich. Thus the
question about optimal level of state bringing maximal utility to individuals remained
unresolved at practical level.

Despite welfare loss from maintaining low level of conflict in hierarchies (that lead
also to decline in social utility), hierarchies are more prepared for implementation of
long term plans. In democratic societies (where elections normally take place once in

6. Conclusions
The society consists not only from individuals, but also from their groups, that form
structures or institutions. At present, institutional economics deals more with verbal
description of structures, while neoclassical economic theory has advance in
mathematical description only of economic interaction between individuals via
market mechanism, but does not consider complementary self-organizing forces that
lead to emergence of stable structures and their evolution. These issues represent
higher level of complexity that requires interdisciplinary collaboration for its
analytical description. Mathematical modelling of structures in economics, their
importance, emergence and conditions for stability is an important problem that is not
sufficiently addressed by economic theories. Physics has been more successful in such
models and it makes sense to borrow some of its ideas.
This paper suggests brief classification of socio-economic structures as well as some
methodology for the analysis of their different aspects borrowed from different
sciences: physics, synergetics, theory of networks. It aims to attract attention of social
scientists to the role of scale economies and non-economic forces in the emergence of
economic structures.
Structures live their own life, creating fields that interact with agents (who are
forming structure) and often have objectives different from the will of some
individuals. Only few structures are sustained by mutual interest, while many of them
are kept stable because of complementary social force – power. Economic structures
perturb individual freedom, and thus we have to reconsider preferences by adding21
some social component to individual. Structures naturally make even a priori identical
agents asymmetrical, and this asymmetry comes from asymmetry in hierarchical
position. Even in democratic societies some hierarchies exist, at least at firm’s level.
The question of excessive tension in such structures is addressed.
It is shown that hierarchies are kept stable by presence of power, and it can be kept by
preferences of some agents for it. A simple game revealing the presence of some level
of conflict in hierarchical structures with asymmetric power level is presented. The
mechanism of non self-regarding preferences plays an important role in power games.
Such games often have equilibrium with moderate level of conflict that is also often
observed in real societies, both at the level of firm and state.
