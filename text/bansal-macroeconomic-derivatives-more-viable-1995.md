I. INTRODUCTION
Recently, Marshall, Bansal, Herbst and Tucker (1992) proposed a class of derivative
products which they called macroeconomic swaps (macro swaps), and a related
class of options which they called macroeconomic options (macro options).l These
swaps and options have the potential to serve as the first effective tools for hedging
business cycle risk.’ Business cycle risk, also known as macroeconomic risk, refers
to variations in a firm’s performance measures that are associated with variations
in the general level of economic activity
The macro swap suggested by Marshall et al. is similar to other swaps in that
one counterparty will pay a fixed price (or rate) on a quantity of notional principal
in return for the second counterparty’s paying a floating price (or rate) on the same
quantity of notional principal.3 What is different in the macro swap structure is
that the floating leg is pegged to a macroeconomic index (or other measure) that
varies directly with the general level of economic activity
Bansal, Marshall, and Yuyuenyongwatana (1994) published findings from the
first study on the viability of the macroeconomic derivatives concept. They chose
as their macroeconomic indices two measures of consumer confidence-the Con-
ference Boards Index of Consumer Confidence and the University of Michigan’s
Index of Consumer Sentiment. While there was no reason to believe that an index
of consumer confidence should be the optimal index to which to peg the floating
rate of a macroeconomic derivative product, the authors chose those indices
because they are not subject to revision.4
Bansal et al. looked at the largest firms in 10 industries and measured the potential
effectiveness of a macroeconomic swap hedge by measuring the correlation of each
firm’s revenues with each of the two consumer confidence indices. The Conference

Macroeconomic derivatives have the potential to dwarf, in terms of transac-
tional volumes of notionals, all other derivative products. Given the dimension of
this potential, further study seemed warranted. This paper offers a second test of
the macroeconomic derivatives (swaps) concept. It looks at the correlation of cor-
porate revenues with gross domestic product (GDP). The viability of GDP as a
macroeconomic index to which the floating leg of a swap might be pegged is dem-
onstrated by the earlier finding that correlations are often maximized when
revenues are lagged. ljall index revisions are complete before the swap's final settlement,
then the revision issue is not the stumbling block itjirst appears to be.
The same 10 industries, the same 10 firms, and the same revenue data used by
Bansal et al. are employed in this paper in order that the results reported here
might be directly compared to the earlier findings. It is shown that GDP is an excel-
lent index to which to peg macroeconomic swaps. For several of the firms studied,
the R2s, even after adjusting for autocorrelation, exceeded 0.90. It is on this basis
that we conclude that the potential for macroeconomic derivatives, as viable
instruments for hedging business cycle risk, is actually considerably greater than
suggested by earlier evidence.

hedging relationships that motivate the empirical analysis that follows. The third
section explains the methodology utilized to assess the degree of explanatory

the results obtained. The following section considers the use of regression betas to
determine the size of the hedge that is appropriate for a given firm to manage its
macroeconomic exposure. The fifth section offers a correction for spurious correla-
tion induced by autocorrelation in the time series data.

B. Results
Aggregate predictive power is maximized with a lag of two quarters. With this
lag, variations in GDP “explain,” on average, 77.21 percent of the variations in cor-
porate revenue. Notice, however, that in the aggregate the results from 0,1, and 3
quarters of lag are not much different at 0.7666, 0.7697, and 0.7717, respectively
Nevertheless, certain of the individual R2 were significantly different with differ-
ent lags. For example, AET’s R2 was maximized with no lag (0.5235), that is,
contemporaneous regression. For BA the R2 was maximized at three quarters of
lag. In every case, the R’s were significant at 0.1 percent (0.001).
In addition to the degree of explanatory power evidenced by the regression R2s,
the direction of correlation is also of interest. The signs on the regression betas indi-
cate that the correlations were positive in nine of the 10 cases. This confirms the

Let’s consider a simple application. Suppose that the R2 between a firm’s reve-
nue and the macroeconomic variable is 0.9598, as it is with HUM using three
quarters of lag. Next, suppose that the regression results suggest that each one
point decline in GDP is associated with a $0.3505 million decline in revenues,
again, as it is with HUM with three periods of lag. Finally, suppose that there is a
linear relationship between revenue and OCF such that each one point decline in
GDP translates to a decline in OCF of $0.1200 million. The swap would then have
to be structured in such a way that the net cash flow to HUM increases (decreases)
by $0.1200 million for each 1 point drop (rise) in GDP This is merely a matter of
properly specifying the reference rate, relative to its initial value, and the notional
principal on the swap.

Harvey (1981).

VI. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
This study has provided evidence on the effectiveness of macroeconomic swaps to
hedge business cycle risk when the floating rate on the macro swaps is pegged to
gross domestic product. The choice of GDP seems more appropriate than mea-
sures of consumer confidence that were employed in an earlier study Neverthe-
less, it seems reasonable to conclude that different indices of macroeconomic
activity might be appropriate for different firms.
The high correlations found for many of the firms studied, even after adjusting
for autocorrelation, suggest that macroeconomic derivatives have considerable
potential to offer a viable vehicle to hedge a firm against otherwise uncontrollable
business cycle risk.
